title: Notes on Icons and Interfaces
date: 2026-05-28
blurb: The Orthodox icon tradition has a theory of images that feels surprisingly relevant to interaction design. An icon is not a picture of something — it's a window into something.
---

In the Orthodox Christian tradition, an icon is not a painting. It's a window. The distinction is theological, but it has practical consequences for how icons are made and how they function.

A painting represents its subject. It stands *for* something. An icon, by contrast, is understood to participate in the reality it depicts. It doesn't point at the sacred — it opens onto it. You don't look *at* an icon. You look *through* it.

## The Interface Analogy

This is, I think, the best way to understand what a good interface does. A good interface is not a representation of the underlying system. It's a window into the work the user is trying to do.

The worst interfaces are the ones that make you aware of the interface itself. Every unnecessary modal, every clever animation that serves no purpose, every label written in the system's vocabulary instead of the user's — these are all moments where the window becomes a wall.

## Reverse Perspective

Orthodox icons use a technique called reverse perspective. Instead of lines converging toward a vanishing point behind the image (as in Western perspective), the lines converge toward the viewer. The icon expands outward. The space inside the image is larger than the frame.

This is a radical idea for interface design: what if the interface opened *toward* the user rather than receding away from them? What if, instead of pulling people into our system's logic, we projected the system's capabilities outward into their context?

## Transparency as a Design Goal

The icon tradition suggests that the highest achievement of an image is to become invisible — to be so perfectly a window that the viewer forgets it's there. The gold leaf, the stylized figures, the flattened space — none of these are decorative. They're all in service of transparency.

The same should be true of interfaces. Every design choice should be in service of the user forgetting they're using a tool. Not through hiding complexity, but through presenting it so naturally that it feels like an extension of thought.

## What Gets Lost

Of course, something gets lost in this analogy. Icons are sacred objects. Interfaces are not. But the *discipline* of icon-making — the subordination of the maker's ego to the function of the thing being made — is directly applicable.

The best interfaces, like the best icons, are not expressions of their creator's personality. They are quiet acts of service.
