Simone Weil wrote that attention is the rarest and purest form of generosity. Not the strained, effortful kind — the sort where you furrow your brow and grit your teeth — but something closer to receptivity. A waiting. An openness to what is actually there.

This distinction matters more now than when she wrote it. We live inside systems designed to simulate attention while actually fragmenting it. A notification is not attention. A like is not attention. These are reflexes dressed up as responses.

## The Mechanics of Distraction

What's interesting about distraction isn't that it pulls us away from something important. It's that it offers us something that *feels* like importance. The ping, the red badge, the breaking headline — each one arrives with a small charge of urgency. And urgency is the counterfeit of attention.

Real attention has no urgency to it. It's what happens when you stop treating the present moment as a means to the next one.

## Attention as Ethical Practice

Weil connected attention directly to ethics. To attend to another person — really attend, without judgment or agenda — is already a moral act. It is to say: *you are real, and I am here.*

This is why the best conversations feel like a gift. Not because anything profound is said, but because both people are actually present. The content matters less than the quality of presence brought to it.

## What This Means for How We Build

If you build software, you are in the attention business whether you like it or not. Every interface is a claim on someone's awareness. The question is whether that claim is honest.

A tool that helps someone focus is serving their attention. A tool that interrupts them to serve your metrics is stealing it. The line between the two is often blurry, but the distinction is worth fighting for.
