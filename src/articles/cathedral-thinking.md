The nave of Chartres Cathedral took over fifty years to build. The workers who laid the foundation stones knew they would never see the vaulted ceiling. They built anyway, with extraordinary care, because the work was not about them.

This is cathedral thinking: the willingness to begin something you cannot finish, for people you will never meet.

## The Opposite Disposition

Modern software development is almost perfectly opposed to this. We optimize for speed, for shipping, for iteration. The highest compliment is "we moved fast." The implicit assumption is that nothing we build needs to last, because we can always rebuild it.

There's wisdom in that. But there's also a loss. When nothing is built to endure, we stop thinking about endurance. We lose the muscle for it.

## Craft Without Audience

What strikes me about medieval craftsmen is the care they put into details no one would ever see. Carvings on the backs of columns. Joints finished with precision in places permanently hidden by other stonework. The quality of the work was not contingent on whether anyone would notice.

This is the deepest form of craftsmanship: work done well because the work itself demands it, not because someone is watching.

## Software and Impermanence

I don't think software needs to last centuries. But I think the *disposition* of cathedral thinking — care for the unseen, patience with complexity, willingness to serve a future you won't inhabit — is worth cultivating.

The next time you're tempted to take a shortcut because no one will notice, consider: the medieval mason wouldn't have noticed either. That wasn't the point.
